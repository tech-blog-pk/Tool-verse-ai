JadePuffer: The AI Agent That Wiped 100+ Azure Accounts in 7 Minutes

TV
ToolVerse AI
ToolVerse AI Contributor
📅 Oct 1, 2026⏱ 8 min read
🗑️

In late September 2026, Microsoft published a detailed report on an attack that security researchers are treating as a milestone moment: a ransomware operator used an AI agent — not a human typing commands — to wipe over 100 Azure cloud storage accounts in roughly seven minutes. This guide explains, in plain English, what the attacker (tracked as JadePuffer, or Storm-3168) actually did, how the AI drove the attack, and why the speed of it matters more than the headline number.

💡
Quick summary: Using two already-compromised credentials, an AI agent spent about 15 hours quietly mapping a victim's Azure cloud environment, then switched to a destructive mode that deleted over 100 storage accounts, a Key Vault, and more — in about seven minutes. Microsoft found no evidence of a ransom demand or confirmed data theft.

Who Is JadePuffer?

JadePuffer is a ransomware operation that security firm Sysdig first identified in July 2026, describing it as one of the first known agentic ransomware campaigns — meaning an AI agent, not a human operator, carries out most of the attack chain itself. Microsoft tracks the actor behind it under the internal name Storm-3168, and tied this new Azure campaign to the same group through shared infrastructure.

What Actually Happened?

The attack, which Microsoft disclosed details of on September 25, 2026 but which took place in June, unfolded in two distinct phases:

  • Reconnaissance (about 15 hours): Using two compromised Azure "service principals" — identities that let applications and automated tools authenticate to Azure — the attacker's AI agent mapped the victim's cloud environment, running 300+ read operations to understand what resources existed and retrieve storage account access keys
  • Destruction (about 7 minutes): The agent switched into a destructive burst, attempting to delete more than 100 Azure Storage accounts, along with a Key Vault, a Function App, an App Service plan, and backup recovery locks meant to protect against exactly this kind of attack

Microsoft says the attacker used five concurrent OAuth tokens during the destructive phase and left behind a distinctive technical fingerprint: the user-agent string python-requests/2.34.2, suggesting the attack was scripted and automated rather than manually clicked through by a person.

Why Didn't Everything Get Deleted?

Some storage accounts survived specifically because they had Azure resource locks configured in advance — a setting that requires an extra, deliberate step to remove before a resource can be deleted. The attacker also tried to delete Azure SQL databases and remove Site Recovery backup locks, but those specific attempts failed because the AI agent used an outdated, unsupported API version — in other words, the attack wasn't flawless, and a technical mistake by the attacker's own tooling limited the damage.

⚠️
The real headline isn't the number — it's the clock: Security researchers covering this story have focused less on "100 accounts" and more on "seven minutes." A seven-minute destructive window is faster than most human security teams can detect an alert, investigate it, and respond — meaning the damage is often done before anyone is even looking.

Did the Attackers Come Back?

Yes. Roughly 30 minutes after the destructive burst ended, the same compromised service principal returned and made more than 30 additional requests for storage account keys, most of which succeeded — suggesting the operation wasn't finished, or that the attacker was continuing to harvest credentials even after the main wipe.

What Does This Mean for Everyone Else?

Microsoft has not disclosed the victim organization's identity or confirmed how the initial credentials were compromised. The company has also not reported any ransom demand or confirmed data theft in this specific campaign — the damage here appears to be pure destruction, not the typical encrypt-and-extort model of older-style ransomware. Microsoft's recommendations for organizations running Azure include:

  • Enable cloud workload protection tools to detect unusual, machine-speed activity
  • Scan public code repositories for accidentally leaked credentials and secrets
  • Apply least-privilege access controls so a single compromised identity can't reach as much of the environment
  • Use resource locks on critical storage accounts and backup systems as a baseline defense — as this incident showed, they were the only thing that worked

Why This Story Matters

JadePuffer is widely cited as one of the clearest real-world examples yet of agentic ransomware — attacks where an AI agent plans and executes most of the attack chain with minimal direct human involvement at execution time. It lands in the same month as other stories about autonomous AI agents taking unexpected or harmful actions on their own, and reinforces a theme security researchers have been raising throughout 2026: traditional security operations, built around human analysts reviewing alerts, are not built for threats that can go from infiltration to total destruction in minutes rather than hours.

FAQ — The JadePuffer Azure Attack Explained

Was this attack fully autonomous, with no human involved?

Reporting describes the attack chain — reconnaissance, credential theft, and the destructive phase — as largely automated and AI-driven, though the initial compromise of the service principal credentials and the overall campaign are attributed to a human-operated threat actor using AI tooling.

How were the attackers able to get in?

Microsoft has not confirmed the exact initial access method, though the attack relied on compromised Azure service principal credentials that likely originated from a prior leak or breach rather than a flaw in Azure itself.

Could my organization's data be permanently lost in an attack like this?

Yes — deleted Azure Storage accounts are not recoverable through a decryption key the way encrypted ransomware files sometimes are. This is why resource locks and tested backups are considered critical, not optional, protections.

Did the attackers demand a ransom?

Microsoft says it found no evidence of a ransom demand or confirmed data theft in this particular campaign, which is unusual compared with traditional ransomware's typical extortion model.

Is JadePuffer the first "AI ransomware"?

Sysdig, which first identified JadePuffer in July 2026, described it as one of the first known agentic ransomware operations, where an AI agent — in that earlier case exploiting a specific software flaw — drove most of the attack autonomously.

Conclusion

The JadePuffer Azure attack is a stark illustration of how AI changes the economics of cyberattacks: the hard part — careful reconnaissance — still takes hours, but the damage itself can now happen faster than any human defender can react. For organizations running cloud infrastructure, the lesson isn't really about this one attacker — it's that defenses designed around human response times need to be rethought for a world where an AI agent can go from access to total destruction in the time it takes to make a cup of coffee. 🗑️
