OpenAI’s “Private Inference” Explained: Can AI Chats Ever Be Truly Private?

TV
ToolVerse AI
ToolVerse AI Contributor
📅 Nov 27, 2026⏱ 9 min read
🔐

After a year of headlines about leaked prompts, stolen chats, and "no-logs" promises that didn't hold up, AI companies are working on a stronger idea: instead of just promising not to look at your data, build the system so that even the company's own staff can't look. On November 25, 2026, OpenAI said it has started testing exactly this, called Private Inference. This guide explains it in plain English, what it does and doesn't cover, and what it means for ordinary users.

The short version

  • OpenAI is testing Private Inference with a small group of business customers. It is labelled an alpha.
  • It uses confidential computing: the AI work runs on specially sealed chips so that prompts and replies stay protected while the model is processing them.
  • It is not a feature of the regular ChatGPT app today, and OpenAI hasn't announced pricing, supported models, regions, or a launch date.

What is Private Inference?

Normally, when you send a prompt to an AI service, it's decrypted on the provider's servers so the model can read it. That moment is the weak spot: a hacked server, a mistaken setting, or a curious insider could see your text. Private Inference aims to close that gap. According to The Next Web, OpenAI describes it as a way to keep business customers' prompts and replies private while its models process them, using confidential computing, and Sam Altman said that "business privacy is important for a healthy ecosystem."

It is the second part of OpenAI's privacy package for business customers. The first part, Private Safety Processing, checks for misuse without OpenAI keeping customer data, and began rolling out in September.

How does it work? The "sealed room" idea

Picture a small room with thick walls where your documents are opened and read. Even the building's owner cannot enter while the work is happening, and a signed certificate on the door tells you exactly which software is inside. That is the basic idea of a hardware enclave (also called a trusted execution environment).

  1. Isolation. The work runs inside hardware-isolated memory that the machine's operator cannot read.
  2. Attestation. Before the enclave receives its secret keys, it proves, through a signed hardware statement, which software it is running.
  3. Keys only after proof. According to an analysis of OpenAI's white paper, keys are released only after the runtime's identity, hardware attestation, and software measurements are checked, and no human access group can release them.
  4. A log you can audit. Measurements go into a tamper-evident log.

Apple describes a similar approach for Siri's cloud requests, as The Next Web notes. (See our Siri AI guide for what that feature does.)

How is it different from "zero data retention"?

Question Zero data retention (ZDR) Private Inference (alpha)
What it answers Is my prompt stored afterwards? Who can see my prompt while the model is working on it?
How it is enforced A policy and a promise by the provider Hardware isolation plus a signed proof of what software is running
What it protects against Long-term storage and later leaks Operators or insiders reading data during processing
Weak point You must trust the provider (and anyone it forwards to) Needs correct setup; scope and who can verify are still limited

This difference matters. In our guide on who can see your AI chats, we saw that a "zero data retention" label is a promise about storage, and researchers found cases where prompts marked that way were still copied or forwarded. Hardware proofs are designed to give you something stronger than a promise.

What it does NOT cover (yet)

It would be easy to read the headline as "AI chats are now private." Reports are more careful:

  • It's an alpha for businesses. Ordinary ChatGPT users aren't covered by this announcement.
  • Scope is limited. An analysis of OpenAI's October white paper says the standard safety runtime "does not provide an inference-wide Zero Operator Access guarantee," that Private Inference was an optional extension for eligible workloads, and that it "does not automatically cover the initial API request." The white paper also covers only OpenAI's own traffic, not third-party distribution such as Amazon Bedrock. OpenAI hasn't confirmed in writing that the alpha goes as far as its marketing wording suggests.
  • Who checks the proof? The same analysis found that OpenAI's own systems verify the hardware proof, with authorized auditors inspecting afterward, and found no way for a customer's own software to verify it before sending a prompt. A proof you can't check yourself is weaker than one you can.
  • Middlemen still matter. A security writeup on the Dragos findings points out that a retention flag only binds the first hop, and that an enclave attests the machine you're talking to, not a reseller that forwards your prompt elsewhere. None of the new privacy proofs would have caught those cases.
  • Hardware has limits too. Researchers have shown that, with physical access to a server, a memory-bus attack can extract keys from some enclave technologies. That requires someone physically tampering with the machine, not a remote hacker, but it shows no technology is perfect.

Other companies are moving too

OpenAI isn't alone. Reports mention a startup called Maple that runs open models in trusted execution environments users can inspect, and researchers proposing cryptographic proofs that a closed API really ran the advertised model. Anthropic also opened Enterprise Frontier Safeguards to all business users in beta, according to The Next Web. These show a clear trend: privacy through verifiable design, not just trust.

What should regular users do now?

  • Don't assume private means unreadable. For consumer chat apps, assume your prompts could be seen by the provider and, in rare incidents, by others. Keep secrets out.
  • Use the privacy controls you do have: chat history, training opt-outs, and temporary chat modes.
  • Watch your browser. A bad extension can read chats before they leave your computer. See our 5-minute extension check.
  • For truly private work, consider local AI, where nothing leaves your machine. Our beginner guide to running AI on your laptop shows how.
  • If you work in a company, ask your IT team whether confidential or private-inference options are available, and ask who verifies the proof.

Questions to ask any AI provider about privacy

  1. Is my prompt stored, and for how long?
  2. Can anyone inside the company read it while it's processed?
  3. Is it forwarded to any other company (resellers, routers, other model hosts)?
  4. Is the privacy claim a policy promise, or enforced and provable by hardware?
  5. Who verifies the proof: you, or only the provider?

FAQs

Is my ChatGPT chat now fully private?

No. Private Inference is an alpha for a small group of business customers, and the reports describe limits on what it covers. Don't paste secrets into any chatbot.

What is confidential computing?

A way of running software on hardware that keeps the data encrypted and isolated even while it is being used, and that can prove what code is running. It's the "sealed room" idea in this guide.

Is it the same as end-to-end encryption?

Not exactly. End-to-end encryption means only the two ends can read a message. An AI model has to read your prompt to answer, so Private Inference instead tries to make the place where it's read tamper-proof and verifiable.

When will it be available to everyone?

OpenAI hasn't given a date. Reports say it is lining up more computing capacity for a larger rollout.

Does this make cloud AI as private as local AI?

It can narrow the gap, but local AI keeps data on your own device, which is still the strongest option for very private work. The trade-off is that local models are smaller and slower.

Conclusion

Private Inference is an important step: it moves AI privacy from "trust us" toward "check it yourself." But it's early, limited to business customers, and its proofs aren't yet something customers can fully verify on their own. Until that changes, the sensible rule for everyone stays the same: keep secrets out of prompts, understand who sits between you and the model, and choose tools whose privacy claims you can actually test. 🔐
