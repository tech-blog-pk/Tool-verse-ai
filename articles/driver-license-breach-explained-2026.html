153 Million Driver’s Licenses Leaked on the Dark Web: What to Know

TV
ToolVerse AI
ToolVerse AI Contributor
📅 Sep 6, 2026⏱ 9 min read
🪪

On September 2, 2026, security journalist Brian Krebs reported that a new dark web marketplace called Nexus had gone live selling digital scans of more than 153 million driver's licenses from the United States and Canada, along with millions of additional identity documents. The FBI has since confirmed it is investigating. This guide explains, in easy English, what actually happened, how it was discovered, and — most importantly — the practical steps you can take to protect yourself.

💡
Was I affected? This breach is linked to identity verification checks, most notably car rentals through Hertz. If you've rented a car or completed an ID check through an affected company recently, it's worth taking the protective steps below.

What Actually Happened?

A dark web marketplace called Nexus began advertising documents on a Russian-language cybercrime forum, claiming to offer identity records obtained from a "major identity verification company." Krebs verified the claim was genuine: the sellers provided him with a scan of his own driver's license as a free sample, and other individuals confirmed their documents were accurately represented in the database.

What Was Actually Exposed

  • 153+ million driver's licenses (roughly 1.1 million from Canada, the rest from the US)
  • 10+ million identification cards
  • 3+ million travel documents and international IDs
  • 579,000+ medical cards, including some dispensary/marijuana ID cards
  • In some cases, front-and-back images, plus infrared and ultraviolet scans, with timestamps aligning to travel or rental activity

Where the Breach Likely Originated

Krebs traced the likely source to IDScan.net, a Louisiana-based identity verification company whose scanning technology sits behind ID checks at car rental counters, retailers, and dispensaries across North America. The company says it works with large brands across a dozen industries, including automotive, banking, gaming, education, and law enforcement. Nexus itself claimed to have been "exfiltrating new data for over a year" before going public with the sale. IDScan.net has said it is investigating; Target has separately confirmed it was not involved.

Who's Been Confirmed Affected

  • Many victims share a common link: they rented a car from Hertz, which uses IDScan for identity verification
  • Notably, a scan reportedly belonging to U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth was among the exposed records
  • Some FBI agents' driver's licenses were also reportedly included in the exposed data
  • Krebs himself found his own license in the database, which is how he initially learned about the breach

Breach at a Glance

Detail Information
Discovered by Journalist Brian Krebs (KrebsOnSecurity)
Marketplace name Nexus (now offline)
Likely source IDScan.net (under investigation)
Investigating agency FBI (New Orleans field office)
Total records exposed 150+ million across multiple document types
⚡
Expert warning: James E. Lee of the Identity Theft Resource Center said this dataset "will continue to have massive value to the cybercriminal community for many years," and Krebs noted a similar service is likely to reappear even though Nexus itself has gone offline.

Why This Breach Is Especially Concerning

  • It's not just a photo: some records include front-and-back scans plus infrared/UV images, which are specifically used to verify document authenticity — making stolen data more convincing for fraud
  • It affects "invisible" infrastructure: most people have never heard of IDScan.net, yet its technology sits behind everyday ID checks at rental counters and retailers
  • It hits high-profile individuals too: the inclusion of a cabinet secretary's and FBI agents' documents shows this wasn't a small or targeted leak
  • The data may resurface: even with Nexus offline, experts warn similar marketplaces reselling this exact dataset are likely in the future

What to Do If You Might Be Affected

  • Check if you've used IDScan-linked services: particularly car rentals through Hertz, or ID verification at dispensaries and retailers
  • Place a fraud alert or credit freeze with major credit bureaus if you're concerned about identity theft
  • Monitor your accounts and credit reports closely for unfamiliar activity in the coming months
  • Be extra cautious of phishing attempts that might reference accurate personal details pulled from this data to seem legitimate
  • Consider identity theft protection services if you frequently interact with identity verification systems

The Bigger Lesson About Identity Verification

SecurityWeek's coverage highlighted a broader takeaway: organizations should design identity systems assuming that identity evidence may eventually be compromised, since a genuine-looking document can no longer be treated as sufficient proof of identity on its own. As identity verification becomes more common — for car rentals, age checks, and account signups — this breach is a reminder that the companies handling that verification become high-value targets themselves.

FAQ — The Driver's License Breach

How do I know if my driver's license was part of this breach?

There's no confirmed public lookup tool available; the safest approach is checking whether you've used services linked to IDScan.net, particularly Hertz car rentals, and monitoring your credit and accounts closely regardless.

Is the Nexus marketplace still active?

No, according to reporting, the Nexus platform went offline shortly after Krebs' investigation was published, though experts warn similar services are likely to emerge again.

What should I do if I recently rented a car from Hertz?

Consider placing a fraud alert or credit freeze with major credit bureaus, and monitor your financial accounts and credit report closely for any unusual activity in the coming months.

Has IDScan.net confirmed it was the source of the breach?

As of this reporting, IDScan.net has said it is investigating the matter but has not issued a full public confirmation; the connection was identified independently by security journalist Brian Krebs.

Why is this breach considered worse than a typical data leak?

Beyond basic photos, some exposed records include front-and-back scans and infrared/UV images specifically used to verify document authenticity, making the stolen data more valuable and convincing for identity fraud.

Conclusion

This breach represents one of the largest known exposures of driver's license data to date, highlighting how identity verification infrastructure — often invisible to the people relying on it — can become a single point of failure affecting well over 100 million people at once. While the specific Nexus marketplace has gone offline, experts warn this data will likely retain value to cybercriminals for years, and similar services may resurface.

If there's a practical takeaway here, it's this: treat identity verification systems with the same caution you'd apply to any sensitive financial account, monitor your credit and personal accounts proactively, and stay alert to phishing attempts that might use this kind of leaked, convincing personal data to seem legitimate. 🪪
