Who Can See Your AI Chats? What “Zero Data Retention” Really Means, and 7 Ways to Protect Your Prompts

TV
ToolVerse AI
ToolVerse AI Contributor
📅 Nov 24, 2026⏱ 9 min read
👁️

Every day, millions of people paste something private into an AI chat: a CV, a client contract, a medical question, a block of code with a password inside. Most of us assume that the text stays between us and the AI. In November 2026, a security experiment showed that this isn't always true, especially when your prompt passes through several companies on its way to the model. This guide explains what happened, what "zero data retention" really means, and how to protect your prompts whether you're a casual user or a developer.

What happened

OpenRouter is a service that lets developers send prompts to many different AI models through one connection. Behind it, the actual models are run by many "inference providers" (companies that host and run models). In July-November 2026, security firm Dragos sent nearly two million test prompts containing unique links and fake credentials ("canary tokens"). If someone opened a link or used a credential, researchers could trace it back to the original request.

According to reports, the tests found evidence of compromises at three hosting providers. On November 22, OpenRouter responded by removing three providers and pulling 125 endpoints from its Zero Data Retention (ZDR) eligibility as a precaution. OpenRouter said the findings "did not show any breach or unauthorized access of OpenRouter" itself and that it notified potentially affected customers. News coverage names Together, Phala, and Novita, although OpenRouter's own post doesn't name the three providers, and the article that quotes it notes that the removals don't equal 125 confirmed breaches.

What the providers said

Not every provider has spoken publicly. Those that have described very different problems:

Provider What was disclosed
Together AI Prompt samples from June–July (requests to MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2 and Kimi K2.6) were stored in a private repository. An AI coding agent committed a credential that gave access to it, and an unauthorized party downloaded the samples. Zero data retention is now the default for new accounts.
Phala An attacker reached management interfaces through a legacy service and took over a GPU server serving DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731. Requests from Oct 21 to Nov 8 should be treated as exposed.
Novita AI From May 28 to Nov 11, requests to MiniMax models were sent to MiniMax’s official API instead of Novita’s own systems, because of a configuration error.
Atlas Cloud Third parties opened links in prompts sent to MiniMax’s API via Atlas Cloud. It stopped sending requests there and removed its models from OpenRouter.

One striking test result reported by Cybersecurity News: a test link sent to a US provider was opened in a company chat in China 24 days later. Some test requests were also copied even though the model rejected them before processing, and some requests marked for zero data retention were still kept or forwarded.

What does "zero data retention" actually mean?

Zero data retention (ZDR) means a company promises not to keep your prompt or the AI's reply after processing it. On OpenRouter, a ZDR-marked request is meant to go only to hosts that declare that they keep neither. But OpenRouter itself states that this list reflects each provider's published or declared policy and "does not constitute a guarantee" that the provider complies.

That's the key lesson: a privacy label is a promise, not a lock. It tells you what a company says it does. It doesn't prove what happens if a server is hacked, a setting is misconfigured, or a request is forwarded to another company.

Why this matters even if you never use OpenRouter

Most people use AI through an official app, but many other tools sit in between:

  • Third-party AI apps and websites that are built on someone else's model. Your prompt travels through their servers and possibly more.
  • Browser extensions and "free AI" sites that you've never heard of, which may log everything you paste.
  • Company tools that route to several AI vendors behind the scenes.
  • AI coding agents and developer platforms that handle your code and keys. (The Together AI case began when an AI coding agent committed a credential. We've covered a related problem in our guide on protecting your API keys.)

The more hops your text takes, the more places it could be exposed.

7 ways to protect your prompts

  1. Never paste secrets. Passwords, API keys, OTP codes, bank details, ID numbers, and private keys don't belong in a prompt. Reporters on this story say the same: keep secrets out of prompts rather than relying on retention settings alone.
  2. Remove identifying details. Replace real names, phone numbers, addresses, and company names with placeholders like "Client A" before asking for help.
  3. Prefer official apps and reputable providers over unknown "free AI" websites and extensions, especially for anything sensitive.
  4. Check your privacy settings. Look for chat history and "improve the model" or training options in your AI app and turn off what you don't want. (See our free-plan comparison, which also covers privacy notes.)
  5. Don't use personal AI accounts for work secrets. If a document belongs to your employer or client, follow their rules about which tools are approved.
  6. Treat "no logs" claims with healthy skepticism. Read what the company actually says, and prefer services that publish clear policies and independent audits.
  7. If you suspect exposure, rotate. If a secret was pasted into an affected service, replace it right away and check the account's activity.

For developers using AI routers or APIs

  • Check for notices. If you received an incident email from your AI router or provider, read it and review what you sent through the affected provider.
  • Know the affected windows. According to Notebookcheck's summary, anyone who sent requests through DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 via OpenRouter, or MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2, or Kimi K2.6 at Together AI should replace every API key, token, and password included in those requests, including ones that appeared only in pasted code or logs, then check those accounts for unauthorized activity. Always confirm against the providers' own notices.
  • Restrict sensitive workloads to providers you've vetted, and don't rely on a ZDR flag alone.
  • Keep secrets out of prompts and out of code that an AI agent can read or commit. Use secret managers and scanning.
  • Limit AI agents' access. The Together AI incident began with an AI coding agent committing a credential. Review what your agents are allowed to touch.

FAQs

Was ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini involved in this leak?

The reports we reviewed describe third-party inference providers reached through OpenRouter, not the official apps of the major AI labs. They also say OpenRouter's findings did not show a breach of OpenRouter itself.

Are my chats in the official AI apps safe, then?

No service can promise perfect safety. This story is a reminder that every extra company in the chain adds risk, and that you should avoid putting secrets in any chat.

What is a canary token?

A fake credential or link planted in data. If someone uses or opens it, the owner is alerted. Security researchers use canaries to catch hidden data theft.

Does zero data retention mean my data can never leak?

No. It's a promise not to store your data. Hacks, misconfigurations, and forwarding to other services can still expose it, as these incidents show.

What should I do if I pasted a password into an AI chat before?

Change that password now, turn on two-factor authentication, and avoid pasting credentials in future.

Conclusion

The OpenRouter findings show that the question isn't only "does the AI company keep my data?" but "how many companies touch it on the way?" You can't control the whole chain, but you can control what you send into it. Keep secrets out, strip personal details, use trusted services, check your settings, and rotate anything that might have leaked. 👁️
