34% of Americans Now Use AI Chatbots for Health Questions

TV
ToolVerse AI
ToolVerse AI Contributor
📅 Aug 30, 2026⏱ 9 min read
🩺

A new Pew Research Center report, published August 25, 2026, finds that 34% of American adults now use AI chatbots for at least one health-related reason — from checking symptoms to understanding lab results. This guide breaks down, in easy English, what the survey found, why people are turning to chatbots for health questions, and an important companion finding about how Americans view chatbots for emotional support.

💡
Quick note: This article covers survey data about chatbot use for health and emotional support. If you're personally navigating a difficult time, Claude can help point you toward support resources — just ask.

What the Survey Found

Pew Research Center surveyed 3,488 U.S. adults from June 22-28, 2026, as part of its American Trends Panel. The headline finding: 34% of Americans have used an AI chatbot for at least one of eight health and medical reasons the survey asked about.

Why People Are Using Chatbots for Health

  • Speed: 28% use chatbots to get health information quickly
  • Symptom checking: 25% use them to figure out what might be causing their symptoms
  • Cost: 22% turn to chatbots because the information is low-cost or free
  • Understanding a diagnosis: 22% use them to learn more about a diagnosis a doctor already gave
  • Interpreting lab results: 20% use chatbots to help make sense of lab results

Who's Using Chatbots for Health Most?

  • Asian Americans: 56% report using chatbots for at least one health reason — the highest of any group measured
  • Adults under 30: 44% report health-related chatbot use, notably higher than older age groups
  • This builds on a related KFF poll that separately found monthly AI health-tool use at 29%, up sharply from just 17% in 2024

Helpful, But Not Entirely Trusted

Nearly all chatbot health users say they find the information at least somewhat helpful, and 47% call the answers extremely or very helpful. But there's a clear trust gap: only 29% say they feel very comfortable sharing personal health information with a chatbot, meaning usefulness and comfort don't fully line up for most users.

⚡
Related finding: A separate Pew report notes that 61% of people who use chatbots for health information also get health information from social media, suggesting many Americans are combining multiple newer, digital-first sources rather than relying on just one.

The Companion Report: Chatbots and Emotional Support

Published the same day, a related Pew report asked a different question: do Americans think chatbots help or hurt people who use them for loneliness, depression, or stress? The finding was notably more cautious than the health-information results — Americans are more likely to say chatbots do more to hurt than help people using them for this kind of emotional and social support. Younger adults were particularly pessimistic about chatbots' ability to help with these needs, while older adults were more likely to simply be unsure of the impact either way.

Why the Two Findings Look So Different

It's worth noting these are two distinct questions with two different answers — informational health use (checking symptoms, understanding a diagnosis) is viewed fairly positively, while emotional and mental health support is viewed far more skeptically. This distinction matters: it suggests Americans see a real difference between using AI as a quick information lookup tool versus relying on it for genuine emotional connection or mental health support.

What This Means for How People Use AI

  • Chatbots are becoming a genuine first stop for health questions, alongside traditional sources like doctors and search engines
  • Trust and usefulness aren't the same thing: many people find chatbot health answers helpful while still hesitating to share sensitive personal data
  • There's a meaningful public distinction between informational and emotional use cases, with far more skepticism toward AI for mental health and social support specifically
  • Younger adults are driving much of the growth in health-related chatbot use, even as they're also among the most skeptical about AI's role in emotional support

Important Context and Caveats

  • This survey measures self-reported usage and perception, not actual health outcomes or clinical accuracy of chatbot responses
  • "Helpful" is a subjective rating from users, not a medical accuracy assessment
  • AI chatbots are not a substitute for professional medical or mental health care, and general information should always be verified with a qualified provider for anything serious

FAQ — AI Chatbots and Health Information

What percentage of Americans use AI chatbots for health questions?

According to the August 2026 Pew Research survey, 34% of U.S. adults have used an AI chatbot for at least one of eight health-related reasons asked about in the survey.

Do most people trust chatbots with their health data?

Not entirely — while many find chatbot health information helpful, only 29% say they feel very comfortable sharing personal health information with these tools.

Do Americans think AI chatbots help with loneliness or depression?

A companion Pew survey found Americans are more likely to say chatbots do more to hurt than help people using them for loneliness, depression, or stress support, with younger adults expressing particular skepticism.

Which groups use AI chatbots for health information the most?

Asian Americans (56%) and adults under 30 (44%) reported the highest rates of health-related chatbot use among groups measured in the survey.

Should AI chatbots replace talking to a doctor?

No — the survey measures how people use chatbots for quick information and understanding, but AI chatbots are not a substitute for professional medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment from a qualified healthcare provider.

Conclusion

This Pew Research data captures a genuine shift in how Americans seek health information — a third now turn to AI chatbots for at least some health-related questions, drawn by speed, cost, and convenience. But the picture isn't uniformly positive: comfort sharing personal health data remains limited, and views turn notably more skeptical when it comes to chatbots for emotional and mental health support specifically.

As AI chatbots become a more common first stop for health questions, this data suggests most people are using them as a quick informational supplement — not a replacement for professional care, and certainly not a substitute for real human connection when it comes to emotional wellbeing. 🩺
