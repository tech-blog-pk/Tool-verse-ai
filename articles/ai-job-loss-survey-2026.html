Only 3% of Workers Have Lost a Job to AI Since 2023: New Survey

TV
ToolVerse AI
ToolVerse AI Contributor
📅 Sep 1, 2026⏱ 9 min read
📋

Nearly four years after ChatGPT's public debut sparked widespread predictions of mass job losses, a new survey finds the reality has been far more modest — and its own author says he was surprised by what he found. This guide explains, in easy English, what the survey actually measured, what it found, and why its results are getting attention from both AI optimists and pessimists alike.

💡
The researcher's own words: Sociologist Jeffrey Dixon, who commissioned the survey, describes himself as an "AI pessimist" and says he was genuinely surprised by how few workers reported AI-related job losses.

What the Survey Actually Asked

Sociologist Jeffrey Dixon of College of the Holy Cross commissioned YouGov, a market research firm, to survey 1,250 U.S. workers between July 30 and August 4, 2026. The 25-question survey asked about current AI use, attitudes toward AI's future impact, and — most notably — three specific questions about job changes since 2023:

  • Have you lost a job due to AI, such as AI automating your previous job's tasks?
  • Have you landed a new job that didn't exist before AI, such as an AI data labeler or AI compliance specialist?
  • Have you received a promotion or advancement you believe was related to AI, such as your AI skills?

What the Survey Found

  • Job losses: only about 3% of workers said they had lost a job due to AI since 2023
  • New AI-created jobs: roughly 6% said they landed a job that didn't exist before AI adoption
  • Promotions: about 9% reported getting a promotion or advancement they attribute to AI
  • Overall takeaway: nearly four years after ChatGPT's debut, AI has not yet produced the substantial job losses or gains many predicted in either direction

Survey Findings at a Glance

Question Share Answering "Yes"
Lost a job due to AI since 2023 ~3%
Landed a new AI-created job ~6%
Got an AI-related promotion ~9%
Sample size 1,250 U.S. workers
⚡
Worth comparing: A separate YouGov poll found about a third of employed Americans are concerned about AI eventually costing them their job or reducing their hours — meaning concern about future risk remains far higher than actual reported experience so far.

Important Limitations to Know

  • Not yet peer-reviewed: this is the basis of an ongoing study, and the results haven't gone through academic peer review
  • Self-selected panel: respondents were YouGov opt-in panel members, meaning their experiences and attitudes may not perfectly reflect all U.S. workers
  • No unemployed workers included: the survey only included currently employed people, so it can't capture job losses where someone hasn't found new work at all
  • Self-reported causation: workers were asked whether they believe AI caused a job change, which relies on personal perception rather than objective verification

Why This Finding Matters

  • It challenges predictions from both directions: neither the dramatic job losses some feared, nor the widespread job creation some hoped for, appear to have materialized yet at scale
  • It highlights a gap between fear and experience: concern about future AI job impact remains notably higher than the actual reported impact so far
  • It adds real data to a debate long dominated by speculation: Dixon himself notes there's been "a lot of speculation" but "little solid data" on this exact question
  • It doesn't rule out future change: a snapshot four years into AI adoption doesn't necessarily predict what the next several years will bring as the technology and its use continue evolving

Different Interpretations of This Data

Some see this as genuinely reassuring evidence that AI's job market impact has been overstated by dramatic predictions, at least so far — suggesting workers, employers, and the broader economy may be adapting more gradually than dramatic headlines often suggest. Others caution against reading too much into a single, non-peer-reviewed survey this early in AI's workplace integration, noting that major technological shifts historically take years or even decades to fully reshape labor markets, and that current numbers may simply reflect an early, gradual phase rather than the full picture. Both perspectives are represented in ongoing discussions about how to interpret this data.

FAQ — The AI Job Loss Survey

Does this survey mean AI isn't affecting jobs at all?

Not exactly — it specifically found limited *reported* job losses, gains, and AI-related promotions among currently employed workers as of mid-2026, but doesn't rule out effects that may develop more significantly over a longer timeframe.

Who conducted this survey and how reliable is it?

It was commissioned by sociologist Jeffrey Dixon and conducted by YouGov, a market research firm, though the results are part of an ongoing study that has not yet been peer-reviewed.

Why did the researcher say he was "surprised" by the findings?

Dixon describes himself as an AI pessimist and expected to find more evidence of AI-related job losses, making the low reported figures notably different from what he anticipated going into the research.

Does this survey include people who are currently unemployed?

No, the survey only included currently employed U.S. workers, meaning it can't capture cases where someone lost a job to AI and hasn't found new employment since.

How does this compare to how worried people are about future AI job loss?

A separate YouGov poll found about a third of employed Americans remain concerned about AI eventually affecting their job or hours, suggesting anxiety about future risk currently outpaces reported past experience.

Conclusion

This survey offers a genuinely useful data point in a conversation that's often dominated by speculation and headlines in either direction — finding that, nearly four years after ChatGPT's debut, AI hasn't yet produced the dramatic job losses some feared or the widespread new opportunities some predicted, at least according to workers' own reported experiences. It's an early, non-peer-reviewed snapshot rather than a final verdict, but it's a helpful reminder to weigh dramatic predictions about AI and jobs against what workers are actually reporting so far.

As AI continues to evolve and workplaces continue adapting, tracking real data like this — alongside the ongoing public concern many workers still feel — will likely remain an important way to separate genuine trends from speculation in the years ahead. 📋
