How an AI-Cloned Voice Tricked a Bank Chairman Into Sending €95 Million

TV
ToolVerse AI
ToolVerse AI Contributor
📅 Oct 19, 2026⏱ 7 min read
🎙️

It started with a WhatsApp message. It ended with €95 million ($108 million) gone from one of Italy's biggest banks — moved out through a chain of foreign accounts before anyone realized the "CEO" on the phone wasn't real.

This is one of the largest confirmed AI voice-cloning frauds to date, and the way it unfolded shows exactly why "just verify by calling them" isn't the safety net it used to be.

What happened

In February 2026, Paolo Molesini — then chairman of Fideuram, the private banking arm of Intesa Sanpaolo, Italy's largest bank — received a WhatsApp message that appeared to come from Carlo Messina, the real CEO of Intesa Sanpaolo. The message described an urgent, confidential overseas transaction that needed his help.

Shortly after, Molesini got a phone call. The caller identified himself as a senior partner at a well-known Italian law firm, confirming that the transaction was legitimate and above board. According to sources cited by Reuters, that voice was an AI-generated clone — the real lawyer named in the call had nothing to do with any of it.

To close the loop, Molesini also received 11 documents, including a confidentiality agreement and a special power of attorney, all appearing to carry Messina's signature.

Believing the request was genuine, Molesini approved a series of transfers totaling roughly €95 million to accounts overseas, mainly in China and Hong Kong.

How the money disappeared

Once the funds landed in the foreign accounts, reports indicate part of the money was converted into cryptocurrency — a step that makes stolen funds dramatically harder to trace and recover, since crypto transactions move across exchanges and wallets instead of through banks that can freeze an account on request.

Fideuram noticed irregularities in the transfers relatively quickly and alerted banking partners and authorities in multiple countries. Through cooperation between Chinese, Portuguese, and Italian authorities, roughly €53–59 million has since been recovered. That still leaves somewhere around €36 million unaccounted for.

Intesa Sanpaolo has been clear about one thing: none of its IT systems were hacked. This wasn't a data breach or a technical exploit — it was pure social engineering, aimed at a human being who had every reason to believe he was talking to people he already trusted.

Why this particular scam worked

This wasn't a crude phishing email. It was a layered con, built to survive a cautious person's instinct to double-check:

  • Urgency + familiarity: The first contact came through WhatsApp, a channel that feels personal and immediate, from someone who appeared to be a real, known executive.
  • A second, independent-seeming confirmation: Rather than relying on one message, the scammers added a phone call from a supposedly unrelated third party (the "lawyer") — exactly the kind of independent verification a careful executive would normally ask for.
  • Paperwork that looked real: Eleven documents with what appeared to be a genuine signature added a paper trail that made the whole thing feel procedurally legitimate.
  • A cloned voice, not a cloned face: Voice is often trusted more than text precisely because it feels harder to fake. AI voice cloning has quietly closed that gap — a few seconds of someone's public speech is often enough to generate a convincing clone.

Milan prosecutors are reportedly investigating a foreign national living outside Europe on suspected computer fraud charges. Separately, ANSA has reported that prosecutors are examining an "identical scam" — the same impersonated lawyer's name — used against a non-banking company linked to BPER Group, suggesting this wasn't a one-off, hand-crafted attack but closer to a repeatable playbook.

Fideuram's chairman resigned roughly a month after the fraud came to light.

This isn't Italy's first AI impersonation scam, either

In 2025, scammers used AI to impersonate Italy's Defence Minister, Guido Crosetto, in a separate fraud campaign that also targeted high-profile figures including fashion designer Giorgio Armani and Patrizio Bertelli. Former Inter Milan owner Massimo Moratti reportedly lost nearly €1 million in that scheme before the money was later recovered.

How to protect yourself and your business

  • Never approve high-value transfers from a single voice or message alone — no matter how senior or urgent-sounding the requester is.
  • Set up a real callback protocol: hang up and call the person back on a number you already have saved, not one provided during the call.
  • Agree on a verification code or phrase in advance with colleagues or family members who might ever ask you for money or sensitive approvals — something a cloned voice wouldn't know.
  • Be suspicious of "independent" confirmations that arrive suspiciously fast — a second call "confirming" the first is a classic layering technique, not genuine reassurance.
  • Treat urgency itself as a red flag. Real emergencies rarely require skipping your organization's normal approval steps.

FAQs

Which bank was targeted?

Fideuram, the private banking arm of Intesa Sanpaolo, Italy's largest bank.

How much money was stolen, and how much was recovered?

Roughly €95 million was transferred out. Authorities have recovered approximately €53–59 million so far; around €36 million remains missing, reportedly converted into cryptocurrency.

Was the bank's technology hacked?

No. Intesa Sanpaolo confirmed its IT systems were not breached. The fraud relied entirely on social engineering — tricking a person, not a system.

How did the AI voice cloning work in this scam?

According to sources cited by Reuters, scammers used an AI-generated clone of a lawyer's voice during a phone call, to falsely confirm that an urgent wire-transfer request from an impersonated CEO was legitimate.

Is this the first case of its kind?

No. Italy saw a similar AI-impersonation scam in 2025 involving the country's Defence Minister, and authorities are reportedly investigating an "identical" scam pattern used against a separate company.

Conclusion

The scariest part of this story isn't the size of the number — it's how ordinary the scam actually looked from the inside. A message from someone you know, a confirming call from someone you trust, paperwork that looks right. AI voice cloning didn't invent CEO fraud; it just removed one of the last reliable ways people used to catch it. As this technology gets cheaper and more convincing, the only real defense left is process: verification steps that don't depend on recognizing a voice at all. 🎙️
