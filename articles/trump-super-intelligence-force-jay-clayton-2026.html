Trump Names Jay Clayton as AI Czar, Launches "Super Intelligence Force"

TV
ToolVerse AI
ToolVerse AI Contributor
📅 Oct 6, 2026⏱ 7 min read
🦅

On October 4, 2026, President Trump announced the creation of a new federal body called the "Super Intelligence Force" (SIF), naming Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton as the administration's new AI czar to lead it. The announcement, posted on Truth Social, came just days after major AI companies signed a voluntary safety accord at the White House. Here's what the task force actually is, who's on it, and what it could mean going forward.

💡
Quick summary: The Super Intelligence Force is a new White House task force with 120 days to study AI's risks and benefits and recommend what role, if any, the federal government should play in regulating it. It's led by Jay Clayton, who keeps his day job as Director of National Intelligence while taking on the AI portfolio.

Who Is Jay Clayton?

Clayton currently serves as Director of National Intelligence (DNI), a role that oversees all 18 U.S. intelligence agencies — a post he was sworn into only two months before this new appointment. Taking on the AI czar role in addition to that job is an unusual dual assignment, and reporting suggests it signals the administration increasingly views AI primarily as a geopolitical and national-security issue rather than purely an economic or consumer-technology one. Clayton fills a vacancy left by venture capitalist David Sacks, who held the AI czar title until March 2026, when he stepped down after reaching the time limit allowed for special government employees.

Who Else Is on the Task Force?

  • Jay Clayton — Director of National Intelligence, task force chair and AI czar
  • Andrew Ferguson — Chairman of the Federal Trade Commission
  • Emil Michael — Under Secretary of Defense for Research and Engineering
  • Scott Kupor — Director of the Office of Personnel Management

The group is also said to include Vice President JD Vance, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, and White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles, with former AI czar David Sacks and former Secretary of State Condoleezza Rice serving as outside advisers. The task force reports directly to President Trump and Chief of Staff Wiles.

🦅
Why "Super Intelligence" and not "AI"? Trump has publicly said he prefers the term "super intelligence" over "artificial intelligence," and reportedly signed an executive order directing federal agencies to adopt the new phrasing — which is why the task force carries that name rather than a more conventional one.

What Is the Task Force Actually Supposed to Do?

According to Trump's announcement, the Super Intelligence Force will "coordinate the Federal Government's engagement with Consumers, Public Interest Groups, Religious Organizations, Critical Infrastructure Providers, and Super Intelligence Companies." More concretely, the group has been given 120 days to study AI's risks and opportunities and report back on what responsibilities, if any, the federal government should take on. Clayton told The Wall Street Journal the task force will examine current law and discuss what actions Congress could realistically take, and said he expects "mechanisms developed in dialogue, just like there are in other industries."

Notably, Trump's announcement was described by several outlets as thin on specifics — it did not outline any concrete regulatory powers, enforcement authority, or binding rules the task force would have over AI development.

How Does This Connect to the Recent Safety Accord?

The task force's creation follows directly from a White House meeting about a week earlier, where AI industry leaders — including OpenAI President Greg Brockman, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, and Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang — signed onto a voluntary package of commitments. Those included in-house model monitoring, third-party safety reviews, and board-level safety disclosures, an agreement Trump has referred to as the "White House Accord on Super Intelligence." Notably, that accord relies on voluntary self-policing rather than binding regulation — and just one day after it was signed, the FTC separately opened a formal investigation into OpenAI and Anthropic over AI agent safety incidents (which we covered in our FTC investigation explainer), showing that voluntary commitments haven't stopped federal scrutiny from proceeding on a separate track.

Why This Story Matters

This is the clearest sign yet that the Trump administration plans to treat frontier AI primarily through a national-security and geopolitical lens — placing the nation's top intelligence official in charge of the government's AI strategy — rather than creating a dedicated regulatory agency or passing new AI-specific legislation. Whether the Super Intelligence Force ends up recommending binding rules, light-touch guidance, or something closer to industry self-governance will likely become clearer once its 120-day report is due, roughly in early February 2027.

FAQ — Trump's "Super Intelligence Force" Explained

Does the Super Intelligence Force have regulatory power right now?

No. As announced, it's a task force with a mandate to study AI and report back in 120 days — it doesn't currently have independent rulemaking or enforcement authority of its own.

Is this the same as the FTC investigation into OpenAI and Anthropic?

No, they're separate. The Super Intelligence Force is a White House task force focused on long-term AI policy strategy, while the FTC investigation is a federal agency's active probe into specific AI safety incidents at those two companies.

Why is the Director of National Intelligence leading an AI task force?

Jay Clayton's dual role signals the administration's view that AI, and particularly frontier "super intelligence," is primarily a national-security and geopolitical competitiveness issue — not just a consumer-technology one.

When will the task force's findings be public?

The group has 120 days from its announcement to report back on AI's risks, opportunities, and the federal government's potential role, putting a report roughly due in early February 2027.

Who was the AI czar before Jay Clayton?

Venture capitalist David Sacks held the role earlier in Trump's term, stepping down in March 2026 after reaching the time limit for special government employees. He's now serving as an outside adviser to the new task force.

Conclusion

The Super Intelligence Force is still very much in its early, structure-setting phase — more a signal of priorities than a concrete policy yet. But pairing a national-security veteran with the country's top AI companies, right alongside an active FTC investigation into some of those same companies, makes clear that Washington's approach to AI in late 2026 is being shaped from multiple directions at once, with the eventual mix of voluntary commitments, investigations, and possible legislation still very much up in the air. 🦅
