AI Company Bankrupt? What Happens to Your Chats (Character.AI Case + 10-Minute Checklist)

TV
ToolVerse AI
ToolVerse AI Contributor
📅 Nov 28, 2026⏱ 9 min read
🗑️

Most of us treat an AI chat like a private diary. We type our worries, health questions, work problems and sometimes things we would never say out loud. But there is a question almost nobody asks: what happens to those chats if the company behind the app goes bankrupt or is sold?

That is not a "what if" any more. In October 2026, Character Technologies, the company behind the Character.AI chatbot app, filed for bankruptcy, and a court-supervised sale is now underway. In this guide we explain what is happening in simple words, what is still unknown, and a practical checklist you can use today on any AI app.

A quick note: this article is written with Claude, an AI assistant made by Anthropic, which is also a company that runs an AI chat service. We have tried to keep it factual and about your privacy, not about any one company.

What happened, in simple words

Character.AI lets people chat with AI "characters". According to court filings reported by KQED and PPC Land, Character Technologies filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in the US Bankruptcy Court in Delaware on October 12, 2026. Its CEO cited high legal costs, difficulty becoming profitable and investor worry about lawsuits. (Chapter 11 is a type of bankruptcy where a company keeps running while it sells assets or reorganizes.)

In a bankruptcy sale, a company's assets are offered to buyers. Here, Disney agreed to pay $20 million for a non-exclusive license to some of the technology. A separate bidder, Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC, offered $10 million for the app, its AI models and user data. A court filing described by PPC Land says the data in that bid includes account and profile information, user-created characters and chat content.

Why attorneys general got involved

On October 14, attorneys general from 39 states and Washington D.C. (reported as 40 jurisdictions) filed a notice asking the court to set conditions for any buyer. Among the nine conditions reported were:

  • real age checks, not just a typed birthdate
  • limits on using chat content to train AI or show ads beyond what users agreed to
  • consent before processing sensitive health information shared in chats
  • access and deletion rights, including a chance for all users to delete their data before any sale

The states also said children may have used the app without proper age checks for years, so a lot of young people’s chats may be mixed in with adult data. They reserved the right to ask for a consumer privacy ombudsman, and a court-appointed ombudsman was later named. Separately, the US Trustee has objected to parts of the sale, and as of November 25 the judge had not approved it.

What we do not know yet

This is important, because there is a lot of scary talk online. Based on what is public:

  • We do not know who will finally buy the data, or if any sale of chats will be approved. A bid is not a completed sale.
  • The company says user information "continues to be protected" under its privacy policy, and that its data practices are unchanged. It has not said publicly whether a buyer would be bound by that policy.
  • The numbers vary by source. The company’s own court declaration reportedly says about 12 million monthly users. State filings cite "over 20 million", and a Kentucky complaint cites more than 21.6 million US accounts created by March 2025, of which about 2.1 million had a declared age under 18. These measure different things (monthly users versus accounts ever created), so treat all of them as approximate.
  • Exporting or deleting your data may not remove it from AI models that were already trained on it. Common Sense Media’s Robbie Torney warned about exactly this.

The bigger lesson: any AI company can change hands

Character.AI is the case in the news, but the lesson applies everywhere. Startups get bought, shut down, run out of money, or change their privacy policy. When that happens, your old chats are an asset on someone’s balance sheet. Big, stable companies are less likely to go bankrupt, but they can still change settings, add new features that use your history, or suffer breaches. (We covered one side of this in our guide on who can see your AI prompts, and another in fake AI Chrome extensions that steal chats.)

Your 10-minute AI privacy checklist

  1. List the AI apps you have used. Include old ones you forgot, such as companion or character apps, photo apps and browser add-ons.
  2. Open the settings of each one and find the data section. Look for words like Privacy, Data controls, History or Memory.
  3. Turn off training on your chats where the app offers it. In ChatGPT this is "Improve the model for everyone" under Data controls. This stops future use for training, but does not erase what already exists.
  4. Export anything you want to keep. Journals, long projects, useful prompts. Some exports arrive quickly, but Character.AI says its export can take three to four weeks, so ask early.
  5. Delete chats or the account you no longer need. Deleting is the strongest step you control. Note that official instructions do not always say how fast every backup copy is erased.
  6. Clear the AI’s memory. Many assistants now remember facts about you. Review the list and remove what you do not want stored.
  7. Use temporary or incognito chats for sensitive topics. ChatGPT’s Temporary Chat does not appear in history or create memories, though it may be kept up to 30 days for safety.
  8. Stop typing what you cannot afford to leak. Passwords, bank details, ID numbers, medical reports and private photos do not belong in any chatbot.
  9. Check if you have a duplicate account with the same email or phone on other AI apps, and clean those too.
  10. Set a reminder to repeat this every three months.

For parents and teens

Character.AI has been popular with young people. If a child in your family uses any chatbot app, sit with them, check the app’s data settings together, and talk about not sharing real names, school, address or feelings they would not want strangers to read. If they used Character.AI, exporting and deleting the account early is worth discussing. Remember that if an app asks for a birthdate, it often cannot check it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini data being sold?

No bankruptcy sale involving those services has been reported. The Character.AI case simply shows what can happen to chat data when a company fails, so it is worth having good habits with every app.

Has Character.AI already sold users’ chats?

Not as far as public reports show. A bid that includes user data exists, but the court had not approved a sale at the time of the latest reports we reviewed. Check the latest news, because this may change quickly.

If I delete my account, is my data gone for good?

Deleting removes your account from the service, but it may not remove data already used in training or in backups. That is why it is smart to share less in the first place.

Does this affect users in Pakistan or India?

The case is in a US court and the states’ filings are about US consumers, but the app is used worldwide. Your data is still held by the company, so the same checklist applies to you. Exact legal rights vary by country.

Conclusion 🔐

The Character.AI case is a useful wake-up call: your AI chats are data, and data has a value to whoever owns the company. You cannot control what a company does, but you can control what you share, what you keep, and what you delete. Spend ten minutes on the checklist today. Your future self will thank you.
