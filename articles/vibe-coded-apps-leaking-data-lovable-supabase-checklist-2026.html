AI-Built Apps Are Leaking Data: 24,000+ Sites Left Databases Open. Here’s a 10-Minute Checklist to Check Yours

TV
ToolVerse AI
ToolVerse AI Contributor
📅 Nov 16, 2026⏱ 9 min read
🔓

"I built an app with AI in one afternoon, no coding needed." It's an exciting sentence, and it's true for a lot of people in 2026. But a recent study found a dark side: thousands of AI-built apps were quietly leaving their databases open to anyone who knew where to look. This guide explains what happened, why it happens, and gives you a 10-minute checklist if you've built (or plan to build) anything with an AI app builder.

What the researchers found

German security firm Crissy Field, with the business daily Handelsblatt, studied 159,041 projects built with the AI app builder Lovable, using pages collected in the public Common Crawl web archive. According to reports, of the 34,545 projects that used a Supabase database, 24,664 returned database records to a visitor with no login at all.

The exposed data reported across these sites included:

  • Over 1,000 identity records, including passport details, from one service provider
  • More than 200,000 chat messages and 80,000 user profiles from a lifestyle forum
  • Tens of thousands of orders and tickets from event organizers and restaurants, with emails, phone numbers, and addresses
  • Contracts, invoices, and personal photo albums
  • Email addresses readable on 7,403 sites across more than 10,500 tables

Important context: researchers say this was not a hack of the Lovable platform itself. It was a configuration problem in how the apps' databases were set up. Lovable has called it a known, industry-wide configuration issue involving Supabase. Also, some of the email addresses found could have been public contact emails or test data, the sample isn't representative of all Lovable apps, and it's not known whether the data was misused. Separately, security firm UpGuard examined about 300,000 domains with signs of Supabase and reported 16,326 databases with readable tables, with more than half showing signs of personal data. In other words, this isn't one company's problem.

Why it happens, in plain English

Most AI app builders connect your app to a database (often Supabase) where users, messages, orders, and files are stored. Your app's front end, the part that runs in a visitor's browser, talks directly to that database using a public key. That public key is designed to be visible. What's supposed to protect your data is a set of rules in the database called Row Level Security (RLS).

Think of the database as a building with thousands of filing cabinets, and RLS as the lock on each cabinet saying "you may only open your own." If those rules are missing or too loose, anyone who finds the building's front door (which is public) can open every cabinet. The AI that wrote your app may have made the app work perfectly without ever making it safe, because working and safe are different things.

The most common mistakes

  • RLS switched off. It was disabled to fix an error and never turned back on.
  • Rules that say "anyone logged in can see everything." A rule like auth.role() = 'authenticated' lets every signed-in user read every row, instead of only their own.
  • A rule that says "always allow." Writing USING (true) removes all restriction, so even anonymous visitors can read.
  • Forgotten places. File storage buckets and linking tables often get no rules at all.
  • The wrong key in the browser. Only the public "anon" key should ever appear in your front-end code. A private, powerful key there is a disaster.

Your 10-minute safety checklist

  1. Ask yourself what you store. If your app holds names, emails, phone numbers, ID documents, payments, or private messages, you need to do all the steps below. Think hard before collecting passport or ID data at all.
  2. Check that RLS is on for every table. In your Supabase dashboard, open the SQL editor and run: select tablename, rowsecurity from pg_tables where schemaname = 'public';. Any table showing false is wide open.
  3. Turn it on. For each unprotected table: alter table your_table enable row level security;. When RLS is on and there are no policies, Supabase's default is to deny access, so you then add the rules that let the right people in.
  4. Make rules about ownership, not just login. A safe rule compares the signed-in user to the row's owner, for example using (auth.uid() = user_id). Rules that only check "is logged in" are not enough.
  5. Search for risky rules. Look through your policies for true or auth.role() = 'authenticated' on any table with private data.
  6. Test it as an outsider. Open your site in a private window while logged out, or log in as a second test user. Can you see anyone else's data? If yes, fix it before sharing the link.
  7. Check storage buckets. Uploaded files (photos, contracts, documents) need their own access rules, and sensitive files should not be in public buckets.
  8. Look at your keys. Only the public anon key belongs in front-end code. Never paste a service or secret key into the app, a chat with the AI, or a public repo. Our guide on protecting API keys covers this in detail.
  9. Turn on email verification and rate limits for sign-ups so bots can't spam or probe your app.
  10. Ask the AI to audit itself, then check it. Prompt: "Review my database and list every table without Row Level Security or with a policy that lets anonymous or other users read private rows." Treat the answer as a lead, not a guarantee, and verify with step 6.

If you already launched something

  • Fix first, announce later. Enable RLS and tighten rules right away.
  • Review what was exposed. Check your database logs if available, and consider whether personal data may have been accessed.
  • Notify users if needed. If you collected personal data from real people, being honest with them is the right thing, and in some places it's a legal duty.
  • Get a second pair of eyes. The researchers' own main advice is to have an expert review access permissions before an app handles confidential data.

Not a builder? What this means for you

If you use small apps built by individuals or startups, especially ones asking for ID documents or personal photos, think twice. Share the minimum, use unique passwords, and prefer established services for anything sensitive. If an app asks for something it doesn't clearly need, skip it. This is also a good reminder that "built with AI" doesn't mean "reviewed by a security expert." The same risk shows up in AI coding tools, as we covered in our article on passwords leaking from Claude Code to public GitHub.

FAQs

Is it safe to build apps with Lovable or other AI builders?

The tools themselves can be useful, but safety depends on how your database and permissions are configured. Researchers describe the problem as a setup issue, not a break-in at the platform. You are still responsible for protecting your users' data.

Is it a problem that my public key is visible in the browser?

No, the public anon key is meant to be visible. What matters is that RLS rules stop that key from reading things it shouldn't. Private or service keys must never appear in front-end code.

I'm a beginner and don't understand SQL. What can I do?

Use the Supabase dashboard's table view to check whether RLS is enabled for each table, ask the AI to explain each policy line by line, and test with two accounts. For anything handling real people's sensitive data, pay for a professional review.

Does this affect only Lovable?

No. The reports describe a broader pattern with apps built on Supabase, including a separate UpGuard finding of more than 16,000 databases with readable tables. Any app with weak database rules can leak.

Conclusion

AI makes it astonishingly easy to build an app, but it doesn't automatically make it safe. The fix for most of these leaks is a few minutes of work: turn on Row Level Security, write rules based on ownership, test as an outsider, and keep secret keys out of your front end. Do that before you share your next link, and your users will thank you. 🔓
