80% of Developers Say AI Coding Feels Like Dependence, Not Help

TV
ToolVerse AI
ToolVerse AI Contributor
📅 Aug 26, 2026⏱ 9 min read
🔁

A new Coddy Developer Survey, covered widely across tech media this week, found that four in five developers — 80% — describe their relationship with AI coding tools as feeling more like dependence than a genuine advantage. The finding has sparked a lively debate among developers online, with reactions ranging from immediate recognition to sharp skepticism about the survey itself. This guide breaks down, in easy English, what the survey found, why reactions are so mixed, and what it might mean for how developers use AI tools going forward.

💡
Worth noting upfront: Several developers have pointed out this is a relatively small survey without fully published methodology — a useful conversation starter, but not the kind of rigorous, peer-reviewed research that should be treated as definitive.

What the Survey Found

According to the Coddy Developer Survey, 80% of respondents said their use of AI coding assistants felt more like dependence than an advantage. A commonly cited driver was the loss of natural stopping points in a workday — moments like waiting for a code review or hitting a mental wall that used to create a natural pause — which respondents say contributes to daily fatigue and longer work sessions.

Supporting Context: Longer Work Hours

This finding lines up with a separate 2026 leadership survey from LeadDev, which found that 45% of engineers now work more hours per week than they did the prior year. While that survey doesn't isolate AI as the sole cause, the timing and overlap with the Coddy findings has fueled a broader conversation about whether AI coding tools are inadvertently blurring the boundaries that used to naturally pace a developer's day.

Why "Dependence" and "Unhelpful" Aren't Contradictions

A common point of confusion in online discussion is assuming the survey claims AI tools are useless. It doesn't. The finding specifically measures a comparison between a "dependence" feeling and a "helpful" feeling — meaning developers can genuinely value what AI tools do for them while still feeling uncomfortably reliant on them, similar to how someone might feel about a smartphone or social media.

How Developers Are Reacting

  • Recognition: many developers describe feeling mentally similar to "scrolling" when leaning on AI for coding tasks, without the same sense of accomplishment as solving a problem themselves
  • Skepticism about the survey itself: some point out the sample size and methodology weren't fully published, questioning how representative or rigorous the findings really are
  • Skill-level nuance: several experienced developers argue the "dependence" feeling is strongest for repetitive or boilerplate tasks, while complex architectural work still requires genuine human judgment
  • Concerns about learning: some developers worry that habitually deferring to AI removes opportunities to build deeper problem-solving skills, especially for those newer to the field

A Real-World Account Worth Considering

One widely shared developer comment described a moment during a several-hour GitHub outage: their first instinct was believing they couldn't work at all without AI assistance — before catching themselves and remembering over 20 years of experience, most of it without AI tools. They were slower that day, but not incapable. This kind of moment captures the core tension the survey is trying to describe: genuine productivity gains alongside an uncomfortable psychological reliance.

⚡
A useful reframe from the discussion: One developer compared AI-assisted coding sessions to the mental state of scrolling short-form video — engaging in the moment, but lacking the same sense of accomplishment as solving something yourself.

Why This Debate Matters

  • It's a genuine workplace wellbeing question, not just a productivity or tooling debate
  • It affects how teams think about AI adoption, since dependence without genuine skill-building could create long-term risks for individual developers and teams
  • It highlights a design challenge for AI tools: how can coding assistants support developers without eroding the natural breaks and reflection that traditionally paced software work?
  • It reflects broader anxieties about AI seen elsewhere too, including recent public surveys showing a rise in general AI-related concern

Reasonable Disagreement: Is This a Real Problem?

Some argue this is a legitimate, under-discussed concern — genuine cognitive dependence and disrupted work rhythms deserve real attention, regardless of whether one specific survey is methodologically perfect. Others push back, arguing that skilled developers who understand a tool's limitations don't experience this "dependence" the same way, and that survey results may partly reflect how a tool is used rather than an inherent property of AI coding assistants themselves. Both perspectives appear throughout ongoing developer discussions, and the debate remains genuinely unresolved.

Practical Tips for a Healthier AI Coding Workflow

  • Intentionally create stopping points, like scheduled breaks, rather than letting AI-assisted momentum dictate an unbroken workday
  • Use AI for well-defined, repetitive tasks while keeping architectural and complex problem-solving decisions genuinely your own
  • Periodically code without AI assistance to keep core skills sharp, especially earlier in your career
  • Review and understand AI-generated code thoroughly rather than accepting it without comprehension
  • Notice how you feel after AI-heavy sessions — if it consistently feels draining rather than satisfying, it may be worth adjusting how you're using these tools

FAQ — The AI Coding Dependence Survey

Does this survey mean AI coding tools aren't actually helpful?

Not necessarily — the finding specifically measures a "dependence versus advantage" feeling, meaning developers can find real value in these tools while still feeling uncomfortably reliant on them.

Is this survey scientifically rigorous?

Critics have noted the sample size and full methodology weren't widely published, so it's best understood as a notable data point sparking discussion rather than definitive, peer-reviewed research.

Why do developers say they're working longer hours because of AI?

Some point to the loss of natural stopping points in the workday — like waiting for code reviews — that previously created built-in breaks, which AI-assisted workflows can sometimes eliminate.

Does experience level affect how developers feel about this?

Discussion suggests more experienced developers often use AI for narrower tasks like boilerplate code or documentation lookups, while newer developers may risk missing foundational skill-building if they over-rely on AI-generated solutions.

What can developers do if they relate to this "dependence" feeling?

Intentionally building in breaks, using AI for well-scoped tasks rather than entire problems, and periodically practicing without AI assistance are commonly suggested ways to maintain a healthier balance.

Conclusion

This survey captures something many developers seem to genuinely relate to — a sense that AI coding tools, while undeniably powerful, can create a subtle but real dependence that feels different from traditional productivity gains. Whether or not this specific survey holds up to rigorous scrutiny, the broader conversation it's sparked about work rhythms, skill-building, and psychological reliance on AI tools appears to reflect a real and widely shared experience among developers.

As AI coding assistants continue becoming more central to software development, finding a sustainable balance — genuine productivity without losing the natural pacing and skill development that comes from human problem-solving — is likely to remain an ongoing conversation worth paying attention to. 🔁
