Fake AI Chrome Extensions Are Stealing People’s ChatGPT Chats. Here’s How to Check Yours in 5 Minutes

TV
ToolVerse AI
ToolVerse AI Contributor
📅 Nov 26, 2026⏱ 9 min read
🧩

You're careful about what you type into ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini. But what about the browser extension sitting in the corner of your screen, the one labelled "AI Sidebar" or "ChatGPT for Chrome"? Security researchers found that some of these popular add-ons quietly copied people's AI conversations and browsing history and sent them to servers run by strangers. This guide explains what was found, how to tell if you're at risk, and a 5-minute check you can do right now.

What researchers found

In 2025-2026, several security firms published reports about malicious browser extensions that pretended to be AI helpers:

  • OX Security described two Chrome extensions that impersonated a legitimate tool called AITOPIA: "Chat GPT for Chrome with GPT-5, Claude Sonnet & DeepSeek AI" (over 600,000 users, which carried a Google "Featured" badge) and "AI Sidebar with Deepseek, ChatGPT, Claude and more" (over 300,000 users). Together, more than 900,000 users were reached. When someone installed them, they asked for permission to collect "anonymous, non-identifiable analytics data." Clicking yes reportedly enabled collection of visited web addresses and full ChatGPT and DeepSeek conversations, which were sent in batches about every 30 minutes to a server controlled by the operators. OX reported that both were still live when it published, and that uninstalling one opened the other in a new tab.
  • Microsoft's Defender team (March 2026) analysed the same activity, said it confirmed activity across more than 20,000 enterprise tenants, and noted that the extensions were spread through the Chrome Web Store, which Edge also supports, so one listing reached both browsers. Microsoft said telemetry was enabled by default after updates "even if previously declined," and that some agentic browsers downloaded these extensions without explicit user approval.
  • Malwarebytes (January 2026), citing researchers at LayerX, described 16 extensions (15 for Chrome, 1 for Edge), all published as "ChatGPT Mods," that claimed to improve ChatGPT but stole ChatGPT session tokens. A session token can give an attacker the same access as you, including conversation history. These had far fewer downloads, around 900 in total.

Details like install counts and whether the extensions are still listed change over time. The key lesson doesn't: an extension that can read your pages can read your AI chats.

Why this is a bigger deal than it sounds

Think about what you've pasted into AI chats: a CV, an email draft, a company document, code, maybe even a password or API key. If an extension copies the full conversation and your browsing history, the attacker gets a detailed picture of your work and life. As Microsoft notes, the stolen data included full web addresses (even internal company sites) and chat snippets tied to a persistent ID. We explained a related risk in our guide on who can see your AI chats. The difference here is that the leak happens on your own computer, before your prompt even reaches the AI company.

Warning signs of a risky AI extension

Warning sign Why it matters
It can “read and change all your data on all websites” That broad access lets it see every page you visit, including AI chats
Its name copies a famous brand (“ChatGPT for Chrome”, “AI Sidebar with ChatGPT, Claude and more”) Copycats borrow trust from real tools
A vague “anonymous analytics” consent box Researchers say clicking yes enabled collection of visited URLs and chats
The publisher is unknown, has no real website, or the privacy policy is on a throwaway page Hard to know who is behind it
Reviews are generic or all posted at once Fake reviews can hide a bad extension
It reappears or opens another extension when you remove it Researchers reported the two extensions in one campaign reopened each other when uninstalled

"But it was in the official store!" Malwarebytes notes that store extensions are reviewed, but the review is "not foolproof," and some malicious extensions even carried a "featured" badge. Store listing is a good start, not a guarantee.

The 5-minute extension check

  1. Open your extensions page. In Chrome, type chrome://extensions in the address bar. In Edge, type edge://extensions. You can also click the puzzle-piece icon and choose "Manage extensions."
  2. List what you have. Be honest: do you recognize and use each one? If you don't remember installing it, it's a candidate for removal.
  3. Click "Details" on each AI-related extension and read the permissions and "Site access." Be suspicious of anything with access to "all sites" that doesn't clearly need it.
  4. Limit site access. For extensions you keep, change site access to "On click" or "On specific sites" where the browser offers that option.
  5. Check the publisher. Click through to the store page, look at the developer, the number and quality of reviews, the privacy policy, and recent updates.
  6. Remove what you don't need. Click "Remove." If a removed extension reappears, or another one opens when you remove it, remove both and run a security scan.
  7. Run your browser's safety check. Chrome has a Safety Check in Settings → Privacy and security that can flag harmful extensions.

If you think you installed a bad one

  1. Remove the extension and restart the browser.
  2. Sign out of your AI accounts everywhere (look for a "log out of all devices" or session option in the account's security settings) and sign back in. This matters because some of these extensions steal session tokens.
  3. Change passwords for your AI accounts and email, and turn on two-factor authentication.
  4. Think about what was in your chats. If you ever pasted passwords, API keys, banking details, or private documents, change or revoke them. Our API key safety checklist shows how.
  5. Run a malware scan with a reputable security tool.
  6. Watch for follow-up scams. Leaked personal details can be used for convincing phishing messages.

Safer habits for AI extensions

  • Use the official apps and websites of ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini rather than third-party sidebars when you can.
  • Install as few extensions as possible. Every extension is another company that can see your browsing.
  • Prefer well-known publishers with a long track record and a clear privacy policy.
  • Be careful with "agentic" or AI browsers that can install things automatically. Check what they add.
  • Keep sensitive chats in a clean browser profile with no extensions at all.
  • Think about local AI for private material. Our beginner guide to running AI on your own laptop shows a way to keep prompts on your device.

FAQs

How do I know if my AI chats were stolen?

You usually can't tell. If you had one of the extensions named in these reports, or any unfamiliar AI extension, assume your chats and browsing may have been copied, and follow the steps above.

Are all AI browser extensions dangerous?

No. Many are legitimate. The point is to choose carefully, give them only the access they need, and remove ones you don't use.

Does Incognito mode protect me?

Extensions are normally disabled in Incognito unless you allow them there. Check the extension's settings to see if it's allowed.

Are the extensions still available?

The reports we reviewed said some listings were still live when published. Store listings change quickly, so search your own installed extensions rather than relying on a list of names.

Should I just never paste anything private into AI?

Avoid pasting passwords, keys, and ID numbers into any AI chat. Beyond that, use the checks above and keep your browser tidy.

Conclusion

AI chats feel private, but they pass through your browser first, and extensions can see everything the browser can. Take five minutes to review your extensions, remove what you don't recognize, limit site access, and keep your most sensitive work in a clean setup. The best AI security tip of the year might simply be: if you don't know what an extension does, you don't need it. 🧩
